Here is how a request moves. The screens you are about to see are the working portal, running on invented test requests. A resident describes the records they want, and confirms an email address with a six-digit code. The request then gets a public number, and a due date on the fifth business day.

Requests are handled one at a time, in a fair order. A person's first open request goes ahead of anyone's second. So one person who files many requests does not hold up everyone else.

Next, the portal sorts the request into one of five outcomes, and writes a draft to match. The most common is expected to be a records search, for a record that is not posted anywhere. Other drafts point to a record that is already public, offer combined figures about students, or cite the exemption that applies.

The first sorting is done by fixed rules. When it is switched on, Claude, an A-I model made by Anthropic, can then search the public record and prepare the draft. Either way, the portal accepts legal citations only from an approved list.

Nothing is sent automatically. A staff member at the Superintendent's office reads the draft, edits it, and sends it. If a draft still has a blank to fill in, the portal refuses to send it.

The public log shows each request, its due date, its status, and every response. Email addresses are never shown. A requester's name appears only if they choose. And text that may identify a student is held back until staff publish a redacted copy.

The software passes fifty-six automated tests. An independent review found nineteen defects, and all of them were fixed. Two steps remain before a pilot. The District's counsel should review the legal citations and the response templates. And the Claude step, tested so far with a stand-in, needs one live test.
