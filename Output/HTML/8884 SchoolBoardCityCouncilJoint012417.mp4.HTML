Claremont School Board & City Council, Joint Meeting — January 24, 2017

Joint meeting summary generated from the Claremont Community Television recording and its speaker-attributed transcript; no agenda, packet or minutes for this meeting are posted in the district's online shares, and the city's records were not inspected. The mayor reported testifying that day with the superintendent for HB 525 on education stabilization grants and read the joint letter of support; Claremont's state representatives discussed the bill; the two bodies agreed that the school board would write a kindergarten lead-screening policy and that a joint lead-education committee would be formed; each side then summarized its budget. The school board's own budget session followed (see the January 24 budget session page). Timestamps link to the same moment in the Cablecast recording.

OBSERVATION A letter in the name of both governing bodies went to the legislature on a prior meeting's consensus; the members first saw its text the day it was sent

The mayor explained at 0:02:42 that at the previous joint meeting "there was consensus from both the board and the the council that I draft a letter", and that she then drafted it. It was addressed to the House Education Committee "on behalf of the Claremont City Council and the Claremont School Board" and had already gone to school board members, councilors and the county delegation by email that morning (0:03:13). She apologized that it was "hot off the press today at about 430 this morning" and read it aloud "for the benefit of the public". Neither body voted on the text at this meeting. Irish said at 0:18:58 that more members would have gone to testify had they known of the hearing.

No statute this page verified requires a vote before a letter is sent in a public body's name, and the letter's substance matched what both bodies had discussed publicly. The point for a reader is that the text of a statement made for the school board as a body was not approved by the board in any recorded vote; the previous joint meeting (about a month and a half earlier, per the chair at 0:01:45) is not in the recorded corpus, so the consensus cannot be checked.

Sources: the recording at 0:02:42, 0:07:15 and 0:08:20. No statute identified.

OBSERVATION The proposed joint lead-education committee would carry Right-to-Know duties once either body forms it

The mayor proposed at 1:04:08 "a committee that has various stakeholders", with members from the hospital, the district, the city and the county, and asked at 1:11:40 for "a consensus among the two bodies to put together that, committee". School board members were to volunteer through the chair and councilors through the mayor. RSA 91-A:1-a, VI(d) in force in 2017 defines a public body to include a school board "or any committee, subcommittee, or subordinate body thereof, or advisory committee thereto", so a committee formed by either body to advise it would need posted notice and minutes under RSA 91-A:2, II. Whether the committee was ever formed, and how, is not in any record available here; a reviewer following the lead-policy story should look for its notices and minutes.

Sources: RSA 91-A:1-a, VI(d) (source note ends 2008, 354:1, so unchanged for the whole 2015 to 2022 corpus): committees and advisory committees are public bodies; RSA 91-A:2, II (2016 codification, the text for calendar 2017): notice and minutes.

OBSERVATION The school board committed to a kindergarten lead-screening policy, with the chair proposing screening as a condition of registration; no statute was cited either way

The chair asked at 0:45:25 whether "The the intent is to have that be a requirement?" and proposed that "all kids that come in had to have been screened to be able to register.", comparing it to vaccination. The mayor recorded the action item that "the school board will be working on a policy to be in place by the beginning of the next school year.", and the superintendent called it "a top priority." The legal basis for making screening a condition of enrollment was not discussed, and this page did not verify any New Hampshire statute in force in January 2017 that either authorizes or bars such a condition. The mayor's description of the state's blood-lead action threshold is her characterization and is not verified here. The policy's final wording, and whether it made screening mandatory, can be checked in the board's later records.

Sources: the recording at 0:44:56, 0:45:35 and 1:11:09. No statute verified; recorded as an observation.

OBSERVATION The officials' account of the stabilization grant cut matches the statute then in force; the dollar projections are their own

The mayor said at 0:02:01 that under "law that had already been passed" the grants would fall "by 4% on an annual basis". RSA 198:41, IV(d), as amended in 2015 and in force in January 2017, provided that the stabilization percentage "shall be 96 percent for fiscal year 2017, and shall be reduced by 4 percent of the amount of the 2012 education grant for each fiscal year thereafter". So the four-percent-a-year phase-out the speakers described was the statute as written, not a budget choice by the state that year. The superintendent's figures (a loss of "almost $400,000 for this fiscal year", over $1 million a year by 2020, 5.8 million over 18 years) are his projections and were not checked against the Department of Education's grant tables. The outcome of HB 525 (2017) was not verified for this page.

Sources: RSA 198:41, IV(d) (2016 codification; source note ends 2016, 85:9, eff. July 18, 2016): stabilization grant reduced by 4 percent a year from FY2017.
