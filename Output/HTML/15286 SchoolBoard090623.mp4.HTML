OBSERVATION Public comment ran about four minutes and was announced as being for non-agenda items only; the policy in the packet is better drafted than most, with two clauses worth a second look

Since September 6, 2022 (one year to the day before this meeting) a New Hampshire school board must "provide the opportunity for the public to comment on school district matters at a meeting of the school board held under RSA 91-A:2," and "the public comment period shall be for no less than 30 minutes." The same paragraph lets a board request but not require advance registration, apply uniform time limits and other reasonable standards applied evenly, and reasonably restrict comments that would disclose student or teacher personally identifiable information or other confidential material.

Here the period opened at 0:06:20 and closed at 0:10:28: about four minutes, one speaker, closed on "Seeing none." Nobody was turned away, no time limit was imposed, and the board's own policy reads the thirty minutes as a floor on the opportunity rather than a required dwell: BEDH paragraph B.3 has the chair close the period "after there is no response to the Board Chair's vocal invitation," and B.4 addresses only the opposite case, where thirty minutes have elapsed and people are still waiting. Whether the statute's thirty minutes constrains the opportunity or the elapsed time is not settled by its text, so no violation is asserted. What is worth recording is the framing: the invitation was "Citizens comments again on non-agenda items," and no separate comment period on agenda items was opened, though the meeting ran two substantial reports. The statute speaks of comment on school district matters without that limit, and BEDH itself contemplates comment on agenda items: paragraph B allows comment "on agenda items or any other District matters," and B.6 gives a speaker a further three minutes on an agenda item following board discussion of it.

The policy is careful, and that deserves saying first. Restrictions on school-board public comment are litigated, and the ones that fail are bans on speech because of the attitude it expresses. The Sixth Circuit struck down a school board's prohibitions on antagonistic, abusive and personally directed comment as viewpoint discrimination in a limited public forum, holding that the antagonistic restriction "by definition, prohibits speech opposing the Board," while upholding the same board's preregistration requirement as a valid time, place and manner rule. BEDH contains none of those three terms. Its speech restriction is confined to "Defamatory statements, comments threatening bodily harm, or other unprotected speech," which is drawn on the constitutional rather than the civility line, and its disruption provisions (shouting, speaking out of turn, obstructing others' view, refusing to stop when time expires) are content-neutral.

Two clauses invite scrutiny. Paragraph B.10 defines "the public" as Claremont residents, district employees, district parents, guardians or students, and news media, and then provides that "Others may speak at the discretion of the Chair or by a majority vote of the Board." Residency-based preferences in a limited public forum are commonly upheld; a residual category resting on the presiding officer's unguided discretion is the part that carries risk, because a licensing standard with no criteria is the classic constitutional problem. Paragraph B.8 excludes anonymous written comment and gives a statutory reason for it: "Anonymous written comments will not be accepted as part of public comment as RSA 91-A:2, II requires that meeting minutes include the names of persons appearing at public meetings." That statute does require the names of persons appearing before the body: but a person submitting a writing is not a person appearing, and the section imposes no duty to reject unsigned correspondence. The exclusion may well be sound policy; the citation offered for it does more work than the text supports.
