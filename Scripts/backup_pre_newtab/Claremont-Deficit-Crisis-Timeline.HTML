A cross-meeting report, not a single-meeting page. How the hole was dug

Strip away the personalities and the deficit has three moving parts, and the district ran all three at once for roughly six years.

  1. Money was spent against federal grant revenue that was never collected. Grants reimburse: the district spends first and files for repayment. Claremont spent and repeatedly did not file. The unreimbursed spending fell back on the general fund, year after year.
  2. Surpluses that did not exist were handed back to taxpayers. Each autumn the district reports its year-end unassigned fund balance to the state on the DOE-25 and MS-25; whatever is not retained reduces the next tax rate. Claremont reported surpluses it did not have, so it raised less in taxes than it had already committed to spend.
  3. Nobody audited any of it. The audits that would have caught both errors were years behind, so each year's wrong number was carried into the next year's budget and tax rate.

The auditor and the board's own chair walked through exactly this on camera on August 5, 2026 (0:54:02), more than a year after the crisis broke. Michael Campo, of Plodzik & Sanderson: "what your business administrators have done or your finance person had done has gone and used a number for unassigned fund balance that they didn't have. So what happens is when you raise the taxes in the community, you're not raising that number, but you're still raising the appropriation, so you're still obligated to spend that money." He added, at 0:55:42, "Nor did you pull back on spending… And then you also factor in that you were absorbing… grant expenditures being counted as revenue that we were getting… Never came in."

Board member Candace Crawford put the grant half in plain words at 0:56:00: "the grant expects you to spend the money and then you turn in the receipts and get reimbursed… And what the district was doing was… they were spending the money and then not submitting the reimbursements. So they never got the money back. And so the general fund had to pay for those expenditures year over year." She called the tax-rate half "falsification"; Campo corrected her on the spot at 0:56:47 — "That's a loaded word. But misrepresentation is a better word."

HIGH The audit lapse was a federal compliance failure, not merely bad housekeeping

New Hampshire's own audit statute is permissive. RSA 21-J:19 says districts and school boards may "hire a certified public accountant or a public accountant licensed by the state under RSA 309-A:8" to audit within one year of the fiscal year's close; RSA 197:23-a requires the treasurer to submit books to auditors only "whenever so requested." Nothing in either compelled Claremont to audit annually.

Federal law did. A district expending federal awards above the Uniform Guidance threshold must obtain a single audit, and 2 CFR 200.512(a)(1) fixes the deadline: "The audit, the data collection form, and the reporting package must be submitted within 30 calendar days after the auditee receives the auditor's report(s) or nine months after the end of the audit period (whichever is earlier)." Claremont ran years past that while drawing ESSER, Title I and IDEA money — which is why the consequence arrived as a federal-funding hold rather than a state sanction. The state-law gap was closed only in 2026, by SB 586 (2026 ch. 272), which repealed and reenacted RSA 198:4-d to require annual independent audits (¶VI), with the audit report due within 9 months of fiscal year end (¶VIII).

Sources: RSA 21-J:19; RSA 197:23-a; 2 CFR 200.512(a)(1); SB 586 / 2026 ch. 272.

HIGH Three consecutive tax rates were set from figures nobody had audited — after the auditor said not to

Under RSA 198:4-b, a district's legislative body — the voters — authorises retention of year-end unassigned general funds "indefinitely until specific rescission," capped at a percentage of the net assessment under RSA 198:5. Claremont's voters had authorised 2.5 per cent, not the statutory maximum of 5; Henry told the board on October 18, 2023, "Yours is only 2.5%, even though the RSA allows up to five. You would have to… put another Warren [warrant] article on to increase it to the 5%." Everything the board does not retain flows into the DOE-25 and MS-25 and reduces the next tax rate automatically.

This matters for how the acts are characterised. The over-return was not a board resolution to mail money back; it was the automatic consequence of reporting a surplus the district did not have. The board's lever was the retention decision, and that lever was small: the maximum retainable figure the board was given was $424,000 in 2023 and $466,664.43 in 2024, so a cap of broadly that order in FY2020 could not have absorbed a $765,333 excess even if the board had retained every dollar available to it. The board's culpable acts are therefore the surrounding ones: adopting budgets and permitting tax-rate filings built on unaudited numbers, declining across nine years to obtain the audits that would have exposed them, and releasing the single buffer it did create (act 9).

What sharpens it is that the warning was given and is on tape. Campo, May 21, 2025, at 0:39:21: "I had conversations with management at the time for 20 and 21, with concerns with not knowing… where fund balance was, was at to please be conservative with… what they returned in terms of use of fund balance."

Sources: RSA 198:4-b; RSA 198:5; recording 16556 at 0:39:21; recording 15357 at 1:09:13.

MEDIUM Every major money motion in this sequence passed on an uncounted voice vote

Of the money decisions above, exactly two carry a countable record: the February 15, 2023 default-budget restoration (a roll call, 4–2) and the September 3, 2025 borrowing (a roll call, 6–0). The $36.1 million budget adoption, the $424,000 retention, its $420,000 reversal, the $350,000 retention and all five trust-fund withdrawals totalling $888,998.42 were taken as "All those in favor? Aye. Opposed?" with no count and no roll call.

The consequence is not hypothetical. It is why this page can tell you which four members voted to restore $600,000 in February 2023 and cannot tell you which members voted to drain the trust funds in May 2025. A voice vote is lawful; the absence of any count in the minutes or on the recording means individual accountability for those decisions is not recoverable from the public record.

MEDIUM The reserves were emptied to pay for spending already incurred

The May 7, 2025 withdrawals are not capital planning. Henry stated the constraint herself — money cannot leave an expendable trust until it has been spent — so each motion converted a completed overspend into a reimbursement from savings. The special education trust alone gave up $425,000 "This amount will offset the overage and out of district tuition for FY 25." Sprague, chairing finance, acknowledged the position on the record at 0:39:25: the funds "are very important to our onward budget planning, and we will be making every effort as we move forward to replenish these accounts."

Three months later the district had no reserves left to draw on. Angell told a legislative committee in October 2025 that "the prior administration depleted all the reserves before the end of the last fiscal year," and the board's own counsel told the public on October 1, 2025 that "there are no reserve funds for Claremont, and the district has already spent down its trust accounts."

What the record does not show

OBSERVATION The three decisive acts have no retrievable video, minutes or vote

Acts 2, 3 and 4 — the tax-rate filings that opened roughly $2.29 million of deficit — are the acts this page can document least. Four separate routes were tried and all closed:

  • Minutes. The district's public Meeting Minutes share carries year folders for 2023–2027 only. The board-packet share and its archived subtree likewise begin in 2023. Nothing from 2020, 2021 or 2022 is posted.
  • Captions. Cablecast holds the recordings, but every pre-2023 school show was checked individually and none has a caption track — the captions.en.m3u8 playlist is absent, not merely empty. A separate whole-history inventory of 316 playable Claremont board and SAU 6 recordings, built the same day, records station captions as absent on every one of these meetings.
  • Transcription. The recordings are downloadable MP4s in principle, but reflect-claremont.cablecast.tv is unreachable from both of this project's shells (HTTP 403 at the proxy), so the files cannot be fetched for transcription without a browser-side workaround.
  • Local press. The e-Ticker News archive covers autumn 2020 and 2021 but reports only the city's tax rate, never the school board's fund-balance decision; the paper ceased publishing around mid-2022, so autumn 2022 has no local coverage at all.

OBSERVATION Conflicts left standing rather than resolved

  • When the over-returns happened. The Valley News (May 30, 2025) and Henry's own presentation put them in FY2020 and FY2021. The Union Leader, quoting Angell, dates the origin to a late-2022 filing to the Department of Revenue Administration. The recordings support both: three consecutive years, not one event.
  • Whether FY2022 ended in surplus. Crawford told the City Council on September 2, 2025 that "the 2022 audit showed a surplus that cleared the deficit." Angell told the board on October 1, 2025 that the FY2022 audit shows a deficit of $198,888. The audit was finalised between those two statements.
  • The FY2021 shortfall. $1,323,810 from Henry's spoken figures; "$1.27 million" as reported.
  • The January 17, 2024 budget vote. Reported as 6–0; the recording carries only an uncounted voice vote.
  • Pratt's severance. $39,500 (most precise, and matching the SAU's own account) against "$40,000" in later coverage. His start date appears as both 2022 and January 2024 within NH Journal's own reporting; January 2024 is corroborated repeatedly.
  • Loan interest. Crawford said 2 per cent and $65,000–$75,000 on September 3, 2025; the Valley News reported $70,000; a later legislative account gives about 1.75 per cent.
  • Counsel's first name. "John O'Shaughnessy" in the earliest coverage and in his own self-introduction on the August 14, 2025 recording; "James" or "Jim" everywhere afterwards. Same Drummond Woodsum attorney.

POSITIVE What the board did that pointed the other way

  • February 15, 2023 — restored $600,000 to the default budget, on a recorded roll call, expressly to avoid a structural deficit when ESSER expired.
  • October 18, 2023 — retained $424,000 against the risk that the missing audits would reveal over-returns. It was the correct diagnosis, nineteen months early.
  • September 2025 — adopted a policy requiring annual audits, and required federal grant documentation to go to board members directly rather than to administrators alone.
  • 2025–2026 — authorised the forensic audit; appointed a member as audit liaison; adopted formal policies on manifests and monthly budget reporting; adopted a budget 0.5 per cent below the prior year.

Two of these four are the reason the record contains its own warning. The board that voted 4–2 in February 2023 to protect against the ESSER cliff, and the board that voted in October 2023 to hold money back because the audits were missing, both understood the danger. The March 6, 2024 reversal undid the second of them.

The reporting timeline

The two reporters cover the same crisis from different angles. O'Grady is at the meetings and carries the tallies, the rosters and the mechanics; Fisher works the documents, the personnel files and the State House. Neither is a substitute for the other, and several of the most fiscally detailed pieces in the Valley News run and the NH Journal run carry other bylines — those are marked.
